The Floating Market

Canal 7, under the Ming arches. Two hundred-odd sampans, rafted three deep, lanterns at bow and stern so the bridge can see what is on the deck. The same families have worked this water for six generations and they will not be hurried by anything that is not the tide.

水上买卖Trade on the water
The market

Nothing here is sold from a building. That is the entire rule, and it is the reason there is still a market to walk through.

When Canal 7 was widened for flood clearance in ’69, the plan came with a covered market hall on the east bank: climate-sealed, indexed, thoroughly reasonable, and drawn by people who had never had to get a crate of wet silk up a ladder. The families who work the water turned it down on a technicality they are still proud of — a hull that can move is a hull that cannot be taxed as a premises. The hall got built regardless. It has been a storage depot for eighteen years, and the families rent space in it.

So the trade stayed where it has always been. Prices are shouted up to the bridge, goods go up in a basket, money comes back down in the same basket, and if you are new the basket will come back with something extra in it that you did not pay for. Nobody has ever proposed improving this arrangement, and the one councillor who tried is still reminded of it at festivals.

What is on the deck belongs to this century. Almost nothing else about the evening does.

潮汐 · the tide

The market runs on river time

The hydro loop above the Ink Canals lets go twice a night. Canal 7 comes up with it, and every single thing down here — when you arrive, when you haggle, when you give up and go home — is arranged around those two moments.

16:40 · Slack water

Rafting up

Hulls come down from the mooring pool and tie up three deep at the second and third arches. Order is by arrival, except for the four sixth-generation families, who take the inside berths and have taken them since before anyone thought to keep a list. Nobody contests it. There would be no point.

17:00 · List posted

The chalk board

Tonight’s goods go up on the board at the north arch in wet chalk. Anything not on the board cannot be sold off the water, and anything on it holds its posted price until the first release. The board is the closest thing this market has to a law, and it lives under a bridge in the rain.

21:20 · First release

Prices come off the board

The canal comes up a metre and a half and the prices come off the board. This is the loud hour: the bridge three deep, baskets going up and down like weights on a loom, someone’s grandmother running four conversations at once. A good hull clears half its deck in forty minutes.

02:45 · Second release

The tide takes the market

The water rises far enough that the arches lose their clearance, and that is that. Whatever has not sold goes back to the mooring pool. There is no bell and no announcement — the river simply makes the point, and everyone agrees with it.

05:20 · First ferry

Empty water

For about ninety minutes the canal is bare from the third arch all the way to the falls. People come down from the Lantern Quarter for exactly this: the only genuine quiet in the district, and the only time you can hear the water instead of the trade.
